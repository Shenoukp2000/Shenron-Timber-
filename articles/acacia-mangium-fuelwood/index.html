Why we build our fuelwood business around Acacia mangium

Heat value, ash, seasoning and supply consistency — the case for building a fuelwood line around a single named species.

Shenron Timber (Pvt) Ltd Published 28 July 2026 · 6 min read

Sri Lanka burns a great deal of wood. Tea factories, bakeries, brick and tile kilns, rubber driers and food processors all run on it. Yet most buyers are handed whatever mixed fuelwood a supplier happens to have that week, with no idea what species it is or how wet it was when it went on the truck. We built our fuelwood line around one species — Acacia mangium — and this note explains why.

The short answer

Acacia mangium grows fast in wet-zone hill country, produces a dense wood with a high heat value and low ash, splits cleanly, and seasons in a reasonable time. For a boiler operator, that combination means fewer deliveries per month, less de-ashing downtime, and a fuel whose behaviour does not change from load to load.

We hold 19,129 standing Acacia mangium trees across our estates, which is what allows us to offer the same species consistently rather than whatever is available.

What Acacia mangium actually is

Acacia mangium is a fast-growing tropical hardwood, widely planted across South and Southeast Asia. It thrives in exactly the conditions our estates sit in: high rainfall, acidic soils, warm year round. On a plantation rotation it reaches usable size in well under a decade, which is why it has become a mainstay of industrial fuelwood and pulp supply across the region.

It matters that this is plantation-grown timber, not natural-forest timber. Our harvesting rights come from Hapugastenne Plantations PLC, a Browns Group company, and every consignment is traceable to a named estate. For buyers who are asked by their own customers where their fuel comes from, that documentation is worth having.

Read across that table and the honest conclusion is that Acacia mangium is not dramatically hotter than the alternatives. Eucalyptus is comparable and denser. The case for Acacia is not that it wins on any single number — it is that it is consistently good across all of them, and available in the volume to supply the same thing every month.

A fuel that is slightly hotter but arrives as an unpredictable mixture is worth less to a boiler operator than a fuel that behaves the same way every week.

Ash, bark and what it does to your boiler

Heat value gets the attention; ash causes the headaches. High-ash fuel means more frequent de-ashing, more downtime, and in bad cases slagging on grate bars and heat-transfer surfaces. Acacia mangium is a low-ash fuel, which is a large part of why it is favoured for industrial use.

Seasoning: the part buyers underestimate

Freshly felled Acacia can be half water by weight. Since firewood is sold by the tonne, buying green wood means paying timber rates for water — and then spending part of your fuel's own energy boiling that water off inside your furnace before it does any useful work.

Air-drying under cover for three to six months brings moisture down towards 20–30%. The difference in usable heat per tonne is substantial, and it is the single biggest lever on what your fuel actually costs you. We cover the arithmetic in detail in our guide on how firewood is priced and weighed.

Why this suits Sri Lankan industry specifically

Our estates sit in the Ratnapura District, in the wet zone. That puts us close to the tea factories of Sabaragamuwa and within straightforward road haulage of Colombo, Avissawella, Kegalle and the Western Province industrial belt. For a buyer, short haulage means a smaller transport component in the delivered price and more reliable scheduling in monsoon months.

It also means we can offer standing contracts rather than spot loads. A tea factory that needs a predictable tonnage every month is better served by a supplier who can commit to the same species from the same estates for a year than by whoever is cheapest this week.
